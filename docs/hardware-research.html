Hardware for Orlis: what to use, what to sell, what to skip

Everything we looked at for running Orlis, tracking bodies better, and whether to sell any hardware. Prices marked ~ are rough and unverified; check before buying. Nothing here changes the beta.

The short answer

Now

Use what families own

A Windows laptop with its webcam, on the TV by HDMI. Improve tracking in software first: clothes colours and a closer look at small players (built, behind Testing switches).

After the beta

Optional extras, never required

Any gamepad on the Orlis pad (Backbone One USB-C recommended), colour wristbands, a wide-angle webcam. Affiliate links only: Orlis stocks nothing.

If a box

N150 mini PC + webcam

Runs today's app unchanged at 1080p. Linux + Chrome kiosk is the non-Windows version. A Ryzen 780M mini PC for headroom.

Skip for now

Own hardware, LiDAR, Apple TV

A Pi sensor bar, depth cameras and LiDAR solve problems we haven't measured; an Apple TV route is a full rewrite. Revisit only if tracking proves to be the bottleneck.

Raspberry Pi 5 (estimate)

Roughly 1.3–2× slower processor, weak graphics: 1080p about 15–25 fps in the heavy games; 720p scaled up about 25–35 fps, no headroom. ±2×.

Intel N100 / N150 (estimate)

Similar processor, and Chrome draws on its Intel graphics: 60 fps at 1080p likely in most games, with room for tracking.

Worth doing anyway

Drawing dominates on every device. Caching backgrounds and drawing fewer particles could plausibly halve the heavy games' cost.

The Orlis pad and controllers

Recommend

Backbone One (USB-C, 2nd gen)

~$99.99. iPhone 15+ and Android, plug and play, comfortable for smaller hands. Say "USB-C version": a Lightning one is still sold.

Budget

GameSir G8 Galileo

Cheaper, USB-C on iPhone 15+ and Android, headphone jack.

Premium

Razer Kishi Ultra / V2 Pro

Kishi Ultra ~$149.99, best-feeling. Kishi V2 Pro ~$129.99 but Android only; its haptics won't work from a web page; not certified with phone cases.

Skip

Razer Edge

An Android handheld (2023, $399.99 Wi-Fi), not a phone controller. Its camera faces the player's hands, not the room.

Plan: make the pad page and the TV menus read any standard gamepad, keep the touch stick for families without one, and confirm on a real Backbone (iPhone and Android) before recommending it. Not before the beta unless tested on hardware first.

Wrist wearables: the PineTime

The best wearable we found is a PineTime with a strap in the player's colour. The strap gives the camera a colour marker (who's who, a steadier wrist), and the watch adds jumps, swings, a buzz on hits and a small screen. It is a premium add-on: plain colour wristbands plus our software cover most of the tracking benefit for a fraction of the cost.

Prototype now

PineTime (original)

~$27, on sale. Accelerometer (no gyroscope, as far as we know), vibration, touch screen, one button, Bluetooth. Open firmware (InfiniTime), so we can stream motion ourselves; Chrome on Windows and Linux can likely connect over Web Bluetooth with no phone or app in between. Adult-sized.

Wait and see

PineTime Pro

Announced at FOSDEM 2026; no date or price yet (one guess: about 1.5x the original). "6D" motion sensor (likely with gyroscope), Bluetooth 5.2, AMOLED screen, digital crown, GPS. Pine64 has paused some products over memory shortages.

Why the strap matters

Matching watch to body

A plain watch leaves Orlis to work out which body wears which watch by comparing motion. A coloured strap makes it instant: the blue strap is on that body, and that body wears watch 1. Standard 20 mm straps, a few dollars each (confirm the size).

What the screen could do

  • Glow in your player colour (a camera marker, and "your turn")
  • Secrets only you see: hider, "you're it", a personal mission
  • Your own small HUD: Dodge health, lives, streak
  • "JUMP!" or "FREEZE!" with a buzz
  • Team or tag colour changes the camera can see
  • What you caught; a tiny Cubo cheering
  • Tap to confirm in Create; join as yourself

Rules: glanceable only (a colour or icon for a second), the TV sends short commands and the pictures live on the watch, screen lit only when it matters, no nagging notifications for kids.

Screens: the pad and the wrist

What the screen is for decides the device. During motion games hands and eyes belong to the game, so a screen must be on the body and glanceable (a colour, an icon, a buzz): the wrist. Between or beside motion (Create tools, a hider's view, secret roles, turn-based games, menus) a real screen in the hands is better: a phone, with a clip-on controller as an option. The phone and the wearable are two halves, not rivals.

What to weigh

  • Does it tie up the hands? Handhelds are wrong for full-body games; wrists are fine
  • Do families already own it? Phones win
  • Kid-proofing: drops, cases, straps
  • Battery and charging across four players
  • Connection: pad web page over Wi-Fi (nothing to install) or Bluetooth from Chrome
  • Lag: fine for both at our speeds
Recommended

The two-part setup

  • Orlis pad = any phone or tablet; a clip-on controller is optional. Encourage old drawer phones as kids' pads
  • Orlis wrist = a colour wristband, upgraded to a PineTime with a colour strap for families who want the buzz and the wrist screen

Together they cover every screen use we've discussed, and the base version costs families nothing.

Selling hardware

No hardware to buy (the plan)

A box plus a pad is too big a price hurdle. Orlis plays on what families own; money comes from the family plan and creators; extras are affiliate links.

Reselling a Backbone or camera

Generally legal in the US (first sale), but: no logos or implied endorsement without permission, keep original boxes, warranty may need an authorised seller, no margin at retail, children's-product labelling rules if sold to under-12s. Not legal advice.

If Orlis ever sells a camera

  • Recommend an existing camera (affiliate)
  • Then OEM / white-label (Luxonis, Orbbec) in small batches
  • Own design on a Raspberry Pi Compute Module board only at thousands of units, never a Pi 5 with HATs in a case

Estimates. Under ~100–120 ms feels responsive; 150–200 ms floaty; over 200 ms sluggish. Tracking on the iPhone and sending only points would be ~80–130 ms, but needs two native apps.

Heat: camera, tracking, Wi-Fi and screen on for 20–40 minutes while charging makes an iPhone warm; long sessions can make iOS slow it down. Help: 30 fps, points not video, dim screen, back off when warm, wired charging. Streamer gear helps too: a TV-top MagSafe mount (~$30–50) plus a magnetic Peltier cooler (~$20–60, e.g. Razer Phone Cooler Chroma).

A Pi sensor bar, if you build one

Parts (~$200–300)

  • Raspberry Pi 5 (8 GB), 27 W supply, Active Cooler
  • AI HAT+ (Hailo, 26 TOPS)
  • 2× Camera Module 3 Wide + Pi 5 cables
  • A rigid 40–60 cm bar, mounts, printed checkerboard

Software

  • Hailo YOLOv8 pose on each camera
  • OpenCV stereo calibration, once
  • Match people across views, triangulate, smooth
  • Send body points only, never pictures

Connection

Wi-Fi for a prototype, Ethernet to rule out Wi-Fi stutter. A single USB cable is best for a product, but a Pi 5 with the AI chip needs more power than a laptop port gives, so it means a custom Compute Module board.

First step either way: let Orlis accept body points from another device (Settings > Testing). That one change serves a phone as a second camera, a sensor bar and an OAK-D.

Borrowed from Nex Playground

$249 box plus Play Pass ($89/year or $49 for three months), about 40–45 games, no making. Worth borrowing: instant player finding, phone-based account setup (a QR code on the TV), and a kid-safety certification (one source says kidSAFE+ COPPA). Reviewers wanted better height recognition, less background interference and steadier hands with two players, which is what our tracking work targets.
